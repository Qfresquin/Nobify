"My problem with agentic coding is that the agent can keep shipping changes long
after you have stopped understanding the system. You start your project and are
full of ideas of what is possible, what your application should do, how the
different pieces might interact, and where this whole thing could go. Maybe you
take the time to come up with a spec for your intended system and its desired
behavior. While doing so, you go back and forth with yourself and/or an LLM to
nail down the semantics, boundaries, contracts, and feature set. At this point,
things still feel manageable.

You have the high-level idea in your head, some kind of written reference, and a
rough sense of what the system is supposed to do and what it is explicitly not
supposed to do. So, you start your work. You create dedicated work packages for
agents to handle, potentially split up into smaller slices so each task is
manageable on its own, without necessarily creating a thousand-line diff. In the
beginning, this works well. Agents perform well. You let them generate tests,
you review their code, or are confident enough in their ability to let another
agent do the review. You might even come up with your own tests and
implementation details on how things should explicitly look.

In the process of working on your application, you are confronted with issues in
behavior, things you missed when specing things out, and areas where you were
simply not explicit enough in your design. No problem yet. You still have an
idea about what your system is supposed to do. This is what your spec is for.
You might adjust the plan and let the agent fix the immediate problem and move
on. And for a while, this feels completely reasonable.

You keep working, and things start to come together into something usable. But
at some point, you reflect on your work together with your agents and might get
a weird feeling. A feeling of being out of touch with your actual
implementation. Not necessarily because the code or the architecture is bad. And
not necessarily because the agent did something obviously stupid. But the system
has kept moving. Specs, edge cases, implementation, tests, and assumptions have
changed. And even though every individual step may have looked reasonable, you
now ask yourself: 'Do I still actually know what this system is supposed to do?'

I purposely want to leave out any broad remark on code or architecture quality
here. Today, I do not want to talk about whether applications built with agents
are good or bad. I want to talk about an approach for not losing touch with your
actual system. I want to talk about using formal models as a way to keep the
intention of a system from becoming just another stale prose document next to
the code.

The project where this became concrete for me is an agentic video editor. The
basic idea is that the editor should not only manipulate a timeline once the
relevant clips are already there. It should also be able to reason over large
media libraries. That means the agent needs tools for finding relevant material
inside potentially huge catalogs of videos, audio, images, transcripts,
documents, and derived features. And it should not only retrieve whole files. If
you ask for a specific moment, sentence, action, visual idea, or section, the
system should be able to retrieve the relevant part inside a file.

So we are not only interested in 'Which media file is relevant?' but also 'Which
segment inside that media file is relevant?'. That distinction matters because
the editor does not just need media search; it requires media search with
something I will coin 'sub-media precision' now.

For that to work, the system has several moving pieces. I will try not to bore
you with the details and limit this to the bare minimum needed to highlight an
issue I encountered. There are four high-level components that make up the
system. Two of them are especially relevant: a Searcher and a Featurizer. The
Featurizer is responsible for making a potentially terabytes-large media
repository digestible and queryable. The Searcher uses these results to resolve
semantic queries over the media catalog. Both of these components, and all other
components in that system, rely on the same holistic meaning.

The Searcher does not only rely on the Featurizer; treating assets the same way
as the Searcher itself. During the development of all modules, these invariants
have to be preserved across the whole system. In other words, if one part of the
system thinks something is a temporal range, another part should not quietly
treat it as a single point. And if one part treats something as point-like,
another part should not quietly turn it into an interval just because that makes
a test easier to satisfy.

This already highlights something I had internalized subconsciously but only
became actively aware of when I started integrating agents into my development
process: Local correctness does not guarantee global architectural alignment.

I want to try and make this visually clear. Imagine our system as a puzzle. The
puzzle pieces have tabs (the outward connectors) and blanks (the properly shaped
holes they slot into). A localized module gives us the shape of each puzzle
piece. This is all fine and good as long as we do not have a second layer of
invariants we want to preserve. We can visualize that second layer as a circuit
grid. The image, so to speak, printed on top of the puzzle pieces.

From what I have experienced so far with AI, and from examples and experiences I
have gathered from other people, AI is pretty good at preserving the shapes of
puzzle pieces. But it gets creative when it comes to preserving the overarching,
non-localized global semantics of an intended architecture. We will get to the
specific bug I want to use as an example in a second, but for now, staying with
this visualization:

What I want is this: six puzzle pieces fitting together perfectly, laid out
according to my specs. Depending on how rigorous I am in my definitions, the AI
might still preserve the local shape of the pieces while deforming the intended
picture on top of them. So the result can be structurally correct but
semantically malformed.

I will give a specific example that I encountered during development. The system
has to distinguish between media that occupies time and media that only has a
position or identity. For example, video shots, audio spans, or transcript
segments can be considered range-like media. Images, still frames, or
representative frames can be considered point-like media. A video shot exists
from t0 to t1. Same for an ASR segment. But an image does not exist from t0 to
t1. It may be associated with a moment, but it is not a duration.

And this distinction matters semantically. A visual frame may be associated with
one moment. An ASR segment covers a range. So, if I want to relate those two
things, the concept is not 'Do these two intervals overlap?'. The concept is
'Does this point lie inside this interval?'. That distinction is small in
English, but it matters architecturally.

So, what happened? An agent fixed a failing test inside the Searcher. Because
tests can sometimes be interpreted in multiple ways, it fixed the test by
collapsing a distinction the architecture needed to preserve. The agent
optimized for the localized shape of the module. It fixed the visible failure,
but it did not preserve the architectural invariant.

And this is the kind of problem that is easy to miss. You could argue that this
is fine because it did not violate the module's local contract. But I have two
problems with that. First, I do care about that. Second, these things compound
over time and can contribute to code bloat and later extensions. Even worse in
my eyes: the diff was not obviously insane, and the outward-facing behavior was
locally correct. The test passed. It made it possible to confuse point-like
temporal information from images with range-like temporal information from
videos. Not externally, but the next agent that has to extend, improve, or
change this module might see that part of the fixed code and suddenly infer that
it is okay to apply that constraint elsewhere.

It is not the dramatic version where an agent destroys your project. It is the
version where it makes a reasonable local change that slowly weakens the meaning
of the system. And depending on your development velocity, things like this
either surface quickly or they slip through the cracks and become part of the
architecture by accident. Before you know it, you might end up with seeds of
weed that might thrive and spread from localized changes over more iterations to
more and more parts of your system.

Agents are good at coding. And generally, I have had and still have a good
experience with them. But at least to my tiny brain, I cannot shake the feeling
of sitting in a sports car at Le Mans with the accelerator set to max by
default. The car is powerful. The problem is not that it cannot drive. The
problem is that every small steering mistake now compounds faster.

To help me with situations like this, I move semantic, architectural, and formal
ambiguity into Lean. Because prose was no longer forcing me to decide what the
concept actually meant. Even more so, prose was not able to enforce it
downstream.

I like Lean and picked it up because it allows me to have a compiled formal
model of my system. For me, that model acts like a condensed view of the global
architecture that matters. Since it compiles, I can add its build step to my
test pipeline and require agents to touch it when architectural changes need to
happen. When they inadvertently try to change something that works locally for a
module but breaks the global architecture, it becomes a compile error in my
spec.

I can take a look at what invariants were touched and reason from there. We got
to stay real here though. Even with Lean, there's no guarantee that the
implementation always matches the intention. But at least I feel a little more
at ease when an agent makes large changes in my codebase.

Either way, coming back to the bug at hand. I made the missing distinction
explicit in the formal model. Before this, the model already had a relation for
temporalOverlap. That relation is for the normal interval-over-interval case. A
video shot overlaps an ASR segment, and an audio segment overlaps another
temporal segment. Both sides have a start and an end.

But the bug was not really about interval overlap. The bug was about a different
relation: a temporal point lying inside a temporal interval. So the formal model
changed like this, and we got to focus on this new relation:
temporalPointInInterval. It is pretty obvious and bears not much meaning by
itself, which is good. A spec is, after all, not intended to be complex. I want
it to be straightforward and simple.

It is the model saying 'Point-in-interval is its own concept'. And then, the
model defines when this relation is even well-formed. temporalPointInInterval is
only well-formed if the left side has a temporal point and the right side is a
temporal interval, or the other way around. And in plain terminology, the law
is: for the same media item, the temporal point has to be contained within the
interval between t0 and t1.

What the agent did while making the test work was different. It added an extra
if statement that coalesced a temporal point into a range interval. The shortcut
was to let the implementation blur point-like temporal information and
range-like temporal information together. The model forced me to name the
missing relation instead.

And once the relation had a name, I could tell the agent to align the
implementation with the formal specification. This rhymed. That alignment showed
up directly in the Rust code. The implementation also got the new relation
intent. And when that relation is lowered into executable behavior, it does not
become IntervalOverlap. It becomes PointWithinInterval.

The actual relation construction now reflects the same distinction. First, the
implementation checks whether both sides are intervals. If they are, this is
TemporalOverlap. But if that does not apply, it falls through to the separate
point-in-interval relation. And that relation has the same shape as the Lean
model: point on the left, interval on the right, or interval on the left, point
on the right. Anything else is not this relation. And at execution time, that
becomes the concrete predicate. Even the boundary conditions now follow the
formal terminology.

So the final code is not just... the test passes now. The final code preserves
the concept. TemporalOverlap is interval to interval. TemporalPointInInterval is
point to interval. And those are different relations with different
well-formedness rules.

I could ramble on about how you can even use the formal spec to let agents
derive semantic tests that aid in preserving the formal model, but that might be
something for another video, if anyone cares.

Why agents change the economics. Agents change the workflow for me. Before
agentic coding, it was much harder to justify maintaining a formalized spec
together with the implementation. Not because the idea was bad, but because the
maintenance cost was real. Maintaining the implementation, writing new code,
changing old code, and adding new or updated tests is already a whole workload
by itself. Trying to keep a model up to date alongside that usual workload was
something I had only done for certain components where correctness and security
were hard requirements.

So in practice, I would often keep formalization more minimal than I actually
wanted. But with agents, that trade-off changed. I can spend more of my
attention on making sure the model is correct. I can focus on the compressed
reference point. What are the concepts? What are the laws? What distinctions
should the architecture preserve?

And once that is clear, I can use agents to help propagate those changes through
the implementation. And that is a very different relationship to generated code.
The agent is not where I want the truth of the system to live. The truth should
live in a contract outside the agent. And the agent helps me keep the
implementation aligned with that contract. So instead of constantly trying to
hold every implementation detail in my head, I can keep coming back to a
smaller, denser representation of what I intended to build.

At least, that is how I stay in touch with the system. The implementation
details still matter, but they become relevant fewer times. They are no longer
the only place where the architecture exists.

And this is where I think this approach fits into a broader evolution towards
spec-driven agentic development. Prose specs are not useless. They are still the
best place to explore the idea. But prose does not compile, and that is my
biggest issue. It can become quietly stale. You can change the implementation,
the tests, and the architecture, and the prose spec can still sit there, looking
like it means something, while no longer being an accurate representation of the
actual system.

Lean does not replace the prose spec. It is orthogonal to it. The prose spec is
where I explore the system. The Lean model is where I force the important parts
of that system to become precise enough that they can fail. Because agents are
very good at producing plausible implementation. But plausible implementation is
not the same as correct implementation. And it is definitely not the same as
architecture-preserving implementation.

So I do not want the agent to be the source of truth. I want the source of truth
to be a contract outside the agent. This is what I care about. The test told me
there was a mismatch. The agent made the mismatch disappear. The formal model
told me whether that disappearance was legitimate. And in this case, it was not.

That is the role I want the formal model to play in an agentic workflow. Not to
replace prose, tests, or me reviewing changes, but to keep a condensed,
reviewable source of truth close enough to the implementation that I do not lose
touch with the system while the system keeps growing.

If you made it this far, thanks for your time. Have a great day."
